    Picard HMC Part I: Gaussian cloud correction

    Recursive implementation route: turn Gaussian-cloud Picard updates into a finite sampler while keeping every adaptive call, cap event and replacement error auditable.

    Researcher reading path

    1. Start with the main theorem to see the accuracy and expected-query destination.
    2. Read Lemma 6.1 to understand why the adaptive request forest has controlled size before the global cap.
    3. Read Lemma 6.3 beside the bookkeeping ledger: each conditional replacement contributes one delta_t, while the global cap contributes epsilon_cap exactly once.

    Source setting and conventions

    \[d\ge1,\quad V\in C^2(\mathbb R^d),\quad \|\nabla V(x)-\nabla V(y)\|\le\|x-y\|,\quad \pi(dx)=Z^{-1}e^{-V(x)}dx\]
    • The source assumes the normalized target pi satisfies KL(mu||pi) <= (kappa/2) I(mu||pi) for every probability measure mu with smooth density, with kappa >= 1. This is an LSI assumption, not a pointwise strong-convexity hypothesis.
    • The supplied reference point satisfies ||grad V(x_ref)|| <= sqrt(d/kappa). No warm-start law is assumed.
    • The strongly log-concave specialization follows only after rescaling the smoothness and LSI constants; the general source theorem is stated under one-smoothness plus LSI.
    • Expected gradient queries include the paper's recursive Gaussian-cloud corrections, implemented RGO calls and cap/fallback accounting. They are not a worst-case runtime bound.

    Size and query count of a capped adaptive tree

    Source: 2609.38710v1. Section 6.1, Lemma 6.1, equations (6.1)–(6.2)

    Open this theorem in the interactive research workspace

    Statement

    A breadth-first request queue with conditionally sub-Poisson child counts and mean offspring at most one quarter has expected total size at most four thirds of its root count and a subexponential upper tail. A deterministic local query cap multiplies the vertex bound.

    • q >= 1 roots; A_0=q and, while A_v>0, A_{v+1}=A_v-1+zeta_{v+1} with zeta_{v+1} a nonnegative integer adapted to the exploration filtration.

    Adaptive conditional-kernel TV error telescope

    Source: 2609.38710v1. Section 6.1, Lemma 6.3

    Open this theorem in the interactive research workspace

    Statement

    For ideal and implemented adaptive algorithms stopped at the same deterministic number of slots, the output-law error is at most the sum of the mean conditional replacement errors evaluated along the ideal capped histories, plus the probability that the uncapped ideal algorithm exceeds the cap.

    • For each slot 1 <= t <= T_max, K_t(H,.) and Khat_t(H,.) are measurable probability kernels on a common history/output interface.
    • Both capped algorithms use the same deterministic T_max and append a measurable dummy symbol after termination.
    • The uncapped ideal algorithm exceeds T_max with probability at most epsilon_cap.
    • H_{t-1} is the ideal capped history and delta_t is the expectation of TV(K_t(H_{t-1},.), Khat_t(H_{t-1},.)).
    \[\mathsf{TV}(\operatorname{Law}(\widehat X),\operatorname{Law}(X))\le\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\max}}\delta_t+\varepsilon_{\rm cap}\le T_{\max}\varepsilon+\varepsilon_{\rm cap}\]

    Complete source statement (Samplinglib restatement)

    For a capped adaptive algorithm represented on a common dummy-filled history space, replacing one conditional kernel at a time changes the final output law by at most the ideal-history expectation of that call's conditional total-variation error.     Average a history-dependent one-call kernel error

    AutoSamplingTheory.TechnicalLemmas.Probability.KernelHybridTelescope.abs_real_comp_sub_le_integral_eventBound · theorem · Teaching coverage

    Read this first

    Mathematical statement

    Let μ be a probability law on a measurable history space A, let K and L be Markov kernels from A to an output space B, and let ε:A→ℝ be μ-integrable. If, for μ-almost every history x, every measurable output event S satisfies |K(x,S)−L(x,S)|≤ε(x), then every measurable T satisfies |(μK)(T)−(μL)(T)|≤∫ε dμ.

    \[\left|(\mu K)(T)-(\mu L)(T)\right|\le \int_A\varepsilon(x)\,\mu(dx),\qquad \left|K(x,S)-L(x,S)\right|\le\varepsilon(x).\]

    Proof roadmap

    1. 1Write both output probabilities as history integrals

      Fix a measurable output event T. Kernel measurability makes x↦K(x,T) and x↦L(x,T) measurable, and the Markov property bounds both functions between zero and one. They are therefore integrable under the probability law μ. The definition of passing μ through a kernel gives ordinary real integrals.

    2. 2Move subtraction inside the integral

      Because both conditional event-probability functions are integrable, their integral difference equals the integral of their pointwise difference. This is the hidden condition behind the paper-level instruction to average the conditional error.

    3. 3Use the conditional budget under the ideal history law

      The absolute value of an integral is at most the integral of the absolute value. Specializing the almost-everywhere hypothesis to T then bounds the integrand by ε(x), and monotonicity of the integral yields the claimed mean error. The result retains the expected local budget; it does not replace it by a worst-case bound.

    Full assumptions and notation

    All objects and hypotheses

    • A and B carry measurable spaces; μ is a probability measure and K,L are genuine Markov probability kernels.
    • The conditional event bound holds μ-almost everywhere and uniformly over every measurable event S at each accepted history.
    • ε is a real-valued μ-integrable function. The event bound itself forces ε≥0 on the histories where it holds; no separate nonnegativity premise is needed.
    • All event masses are finite probabilities, so conversion from extended nonnegative values to real values loses no information.

    Mathematical proof

    1. Write both output probabilities as history integrals

    Fix a measurable output event T. Kernel measurability makes x↦K(x,T) and x↦L(x,T) measurable, and the Markov property bounds both functions between zero and one. They are therefore integrable under the probability law μ. The definition of passing μ through a kernel gives ordinary real integrals.

    \[(\mu K)(T)=\int_A K(x,T)\,\mu(dx),\qquad (\mu L)(T)=\int_A L(x,T)\,\mu(dx).\]
    Corresponding Lean step

    The local hint proves integrability from Kernel.measurable_coe and measureReal_le_one. The local happly unfolds Measure.bind, uses Measure.bind_apply, and invokes integral_toReal after proving every conditional event has finite mass.

    2. Move subtraction inside the integral

    Because both conditional event-probability functions are integrable, their integral difference equals the integral of their pointwise difference. This is the hidden condition behind the paper-level instruction to average the conditional error.

    \[(\mu K)(T)-(\mu L)(T)=\int_A\bigl(K(x,T)-L(x,T)\bigr)\,\mu(dx).\]
    Corresponding Lean step

    integral_sub is applied only after hint K and hint L have supplied both integrability witnesses.

    3. Use the conditional budget under the ideal history law

    The absolute value of an integral is at most the integral of the absolute value. Specializing the almost-everywhere hypothesis to T then bounds the integrand by ε(x), and monotonicity of the integral yields the claimed mean error. The result retains the expected local budget; it does not replace it by a worst-case bound.

    \[\left|\int_A(K(x,T)-L(x,T))\,d\mu\right|\le\int_A|K(x,T)-L(x,T)|\,d\mu\le\int_A\varepsilon(x)\,d\mu.\]
    Corresponding Lean step

    abs_integral_le_integral_abs supplies the first inequality. Integrable.abs and integral_mono_ae use hbound specialized to T for the second.

      Read this first

      Mathematical statement

      For real sequences a and δ and a natural number n, if every adjacent difference |a(i+1)−a(i)| is at most δ(i) for i<n, then the endpoint difference |a(n)−a(0)| is at most the sum of those n budgets.

      \[\bigl|a_n-a_0\bigr|\le\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\delta_i\quad\text{whenever}\quad |a_{i+1}-a_i|\le\delta_i.\]

      Proof roadmap

      1. 1Split the last endpoint difference

        Insert the penultimate value a_n between a_{n+1} and a_0. The real triangle inequality separates the newest replacement error from the accumulated earlier error.

      2. 2Apply the newest budget and the induction hypothesis

        The step assumption bounds the first term by δ_n. The induction hypothesis bounds the second by the sum through n−1. Appending δ_n gives exactly the finite range sum through n.

      Full assumptions and notation

      All objects and hypotheses

      • The hybrid chain has a fixed finite length n; this theorem says nothing about a random or infinite number of calls.

      1. Split the last endpoint difference

      \[|a_{n+1}-a_0|\le|a_{n+1}-a_n|+|a_n-a_0|.\]
