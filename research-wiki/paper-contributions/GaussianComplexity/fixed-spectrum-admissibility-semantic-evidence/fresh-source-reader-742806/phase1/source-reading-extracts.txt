1 Introduction We study the problem of sampling from a centered Gaussian distribution given query access to its
unnormalized density.
Gaussian sampling is a fundamental algorithmic task
with applications in Bayesian inference [ 7 ] ,
numerical simulation [ 11 ] ,
and randomized approximation algorithms [ 16 ] . The target is $\pi_{A}=N(0,A^{-1})$ , where the unknown precision
matrix $A$ satisfies $I_{d}\preceq A\preceq\kappa I_{d}$ .
An algorithm accesses $A$ through matrix–vector product queries.
At each step, it chooses a vector $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ and observes $Ax$ .
The goal of the algorithm is to output a random vector whose distribution
is within $\varepsilon$ of $\pi_{A}$ in total variation (TV) distance.
We ask how many queries required for sampling from $d$ -dimensional Gaussian distribution to accuracy $\varepsilon$ with the condition number $\kappa$ . Gaussian sampling has a long history, with methods based on polynomial
approximations to matrix square roots and inverse square
roots [ 13 , 12 ] ,
Krylov subspaces and conjugate
gradients [ 23 , 4 , 11 ] ,
and Markov chain Monte Carlo, including Gibbs sampling and Hamiltonian
Monte Carlo [ 25 , 14 , 2 ] .
In the first-order oracle model considered here, [ 17 ] obtained a
query bound of $O(\kappa d\log^{3}(\tfrac{\kappa d}{\varepsilon}))$ for Metropolized Hamiltonian Monte Carlo.
For Gaussian targets, [ 2 ] improved this to $\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{\kappa}\,d^{1/4}\log(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}))$ using long, randomized integration times, with initialization included
in the query count.
Analyses of Hit-and-Run also yield an $O(\kappa d\log(\tfrac{\kappa d}{\varepsilon}))$ query bound, since each exact Gaussian line update requires only one
matrix-vector
query [ 3 , 8 ] .
A sharper worst-case upper bound is provided by the polynomial sampler
of [ 10 ] , which requires $O(\min\{d,\sqrt{\kappa}\log(\tfrac{d}{\varepsilon})\})$ queries for total variation error $\varepsilon$ .
At a sufficiently small constant total variation error,
they proved a lower bound of $\Omega(\min\{d,\sqrt{\kappa}\})$ against arbitrary randomized adaptive algorithms.
They further obtained $\Omega_{\delta}(\sqrt{\kappa}\log d)$ when $\kappa\leq d^{1/5-\delta}$ ,
for any fixed $\delta\in(0,1/5)$ ,
matching their upper bound at constant accuracy in this regime.
They conjectured that $\Omega(\sqrt{\kappa}\log d)$ queries are necessary whenever $\sqrt{\kappa}\log d\leq d$ . A recent preprint [ 22 ] gives an $\Omega(\log d)$ lower bound for $\kappa=2$ and $\varepsilon=\frac{1}{10}$ , using instances with a fixed spectrum and a random
eigenbasis. None of these lower bounds grows as $\varepsilon\to 0$ , and for $\kappa\geq d^{1/5}$ they
are below the upper bound by a factor of order $\log(d/\varepsilon)$ . However, both regimes arise in applications.
Large condition numbers arise naturally in spatial statistics and Gaussian process regression [ 1 , 6 , 18 ] .
On a bounded domain, the covariance matrices of a fixed Matérn model satisfy $\kappa=\Omega(d)$ as the number $d$ of sampling locations increases [ 26 ] .
Sparse precision matrices arising from SPDE-based Gaussian Markov random field approximations of Mat’ern fields can likewise become ill-conditioned under mesh refinement [ 19 , 24 ] .
For example, a regular-grid discretization of the Matérn SPDE with $\nu=1$ on a fixed two-dimensional periodic domain satisfies $\kappa=\Theta(d^{2})$ under uniform refinement. High sampling accuracy matters for estimating a rare failure probability $p$ [ 20 ] , since TV error $\varepsilon$ allows relative bias up to $\varepsilon/p$ .
High accuracy matters in Gibbs samplers with Gaussian conditionals [ 7 , 21 ] , where uncorrected sampling errors can bias the chain [ 15 ] .
From a common initial law, a uniform per-update TV error of $\varepsilon$ accumulates to at most $N\varepsilon$ over $N$ updates.
In neither regime do the known bounds determine the query complexity.
This leads to the following question. Can we establish sharp lower bounds for high-accuracy sampling from ill-conditioned Gaussians, uniformly over all dimensions ?

For every fixed $\delta\in(0,1)$ , sufficiently large $d$ , $2\leq\kappa\leq d^{1-\delta}$ , and $0<\varepsilon\leq 1/10$ , any Gaussian sampler requires $\Omega_{\delta}(\sqrt{\kappa}\log d)$ queries. This bound is tight at constant accuracy and, more generally, whenever $d^{-C}\leq\varepsilon\leq 1/10$ for a fixed $C>0$ , with matching constants depending on $\delta$ and $C$ .
For $\kappa\geq 4$ and $\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{16}$ it is implied by Theorem 3.1 while the new
cases are $2\leq\kappa<4$ and $\frac{1}{16}<\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{10}$ . We include it because its
hard instances have a fixed spectrum, so the bound holds even for algorithms that know the spectrum
of the precision matrix.
Section 4.4 explains why the analysis of these instances gives no dependence on $\varepsilon$ and does not extend beyond $\kappa=d^{1-\delta}$ . Remark 1.3 ( Connection to strongly log-concave sampling). Our lower bounds also apply to sampling from $\pi\propto e^{-V}$ with $I_{d}\preceq\nabla^{2}V\preceq\kappa I_{d}$ using exact
value-and-gradient queries, even when the minimizer is known.
For $\kappa=2$ and TV accuracy $1/10$ , [ 22 ] establishes an $O_{\eta}(d^{\eta})$ upper bound for every fixed $\eta>0$ ,
leaving a gap from the $\Omega(\log d)$ lower bound, whereas
Gaussian sampling has complexity $\Theta(\log d)$ in this regime.
For general condition numbers and high accuracy, [ 9 ] obtain TV error $\varepsilon$ using $\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{\kappa}\,d^{1/4}\operatorname{polylog}(1/\varepsilon))$ expected gradient queries with a warm start.
Thus, our results provide an improved lower bounds for general strongly log-concave sampling with almost tight lower bounds for Gaussian subclass.

2 Preliminaries We write $\|\cdot\|$ for the Euclidean norm and $I_{d}$ for the $d\times d$ identity matrix.
For symmetric matrices $A$ and $B$ , the notation $A\preceq B$ means that $B-A$ is positive semidefinite. Let $O(n):=\{O\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:O^{\top}O=I_{n}\}$ denote the real orthogonal group, equipped with Haar probability
measure.
It has two components, $\{\det O=+1\}$ , $\{\det O=-1\}$ , and each have Haar probability $1/2$ .
For $b\in\{-1,+1\}$ , we write $\mu_{n,b}$ for Haar probability
measure conditioned on $\det O=b$ . Targets and oracle. We restrict the target class to centered Gaussians.
This restriction removes the hardness of locating the target and isolates the difficulty of sampling with an unknown covariance.
For an integer $d\geq 1$ and a parameter $\kappa\geq 1$ , without loss of generality, we normalize the spectral bounds to $1$ and $\kappa$ by rescaling and consider
the precision class $\mathcal{A}_{d,\kappa}:=\left\{A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}:A=A^{\top},\quad I_{d}\preceq A\preceq\kappa I_{d}\right\}.$ An input $A\in\mathcal{A}_{d,\kappa}$ specifies the quadratic
potential $f_{A}(x):=\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}Ax$ and the target distribution $\pi_{A}:=N(0,A^{-1})$ .
At a point $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ , the exact first-order oracle returns $\operatorname{FO}_{A}(x):=\bigl(f_{A}(x),\nabla f_{A}(x)\bigr)=\left(\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}Ax,Ax\right).$ (1) On this class, exact first-order access is equivalent to exact
matrix–vector access.
Indeed, a first-order response contains $Ax$ , while a
matrix–vector response determines both the gradient $Ax$ and the function value $\frac{1}{2}\langle x,Ax\rangle$ .
Thus the exact value-and-gradient, gradient-only, and
matrix–vector query complexities coincide. Randomized algorithms. A randomized algorithm chooses its queries and stopping decisions
as Borel functions of its internal randomness and previous oracle
responses, where the randomness is independent of the input.
Its output may be any $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ -valued Borel function of
the same information.
The algorithm has fixed query budget $T$ if it makes
at most $T$ queries for every admissible input and every
realization of its internal randomness. Accuracy measures. For probability measures $P$ and $Q$ on a common measurable space,
their total variation distance is $\operatorname{TV}(P,Q):=\sup_{B}|P(B)-Q(B)|=\frac{1}{2}\int|p-q|\,d\lambda,$ where the supremum ranges over measurable sets, and $p$ and $q$ are the densities of $P$ and $Q$ with respect to any common
dominating measure $\lambda$ .
In particular, $|\mathbb{E}_{P}h-\mathbb{E}_{Q}h|\leq\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)$ for every measurable function $h$ taking values in $[0,1]$ . Sampling accuracy and query complexity. For a fixed input $A$ , let $\rho_{A}^{\mathrm{Alg}}$ denote
the law of the algorithm’s output, with all internal
randomness included.
For $0<\varepsilon<1$ , define $Q_{G}(d,\kappa,\varepsilon):=\inf\left\{T\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}:\begin{array}[]{l}\exists\,\mathrm{Alg}\text{ with fixed query budget }T\\[2.0pt]
\text{such that }\displaystyle\sup_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{d,\kappa}}\operatorname{TV}\bigl(\rho_{A}^{\mathrm{Alg}},\pi_{A}\bigr)\leq\varepsilon\end{array}\right\}.$ (2) The accuracy requirement is uniform over inputs and concerns
the output law after averaging over the algorithm’s seed.
Throughout the paper, query budgets are deterministic caps
on the number of queries in each run, rather than bounds
on the expected number of queries.

4 Lower Bound via Hidden Eigenbasis The preprint [ 22 ] proved an $\Omega(\log d)$ query lower bound for centered Gaussian
sampling with $I_{d}\preceq A\preceq 2I_{d}$ at total variation accuracy $\varepsilon=\frac{1}{10}$ .
We subsequently examine the limitations of this analysis for establishing accuracy-dependent lower bounds in Section 4.4 . Theorem 4.1 ( Extension to general $\kappa$ ). For every fixed $\delta\in(0,1)$ , there exists $d_{0}(\delta)$ such
that, for every integer $d\geq d_{0}(\delta)$ , $2\leq\kappa\leq d^{1-\delta}$ , and $0<\varepsilon\leq 1/10$ , $Q_{G}(d,\kappa,\varepsilon)\geq\frac{\delta}{256}\sqrt{\kappa}\log d.$ Compared with Theorem 3.1 , this bound also covers $2\leq\kappa<4$ and $\frac{1}{16}<\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{10}$ , and it holds even when the spectrum of $A$ is known to
the algorithm. On the other hand, it does not grow as $\varepsilon\to 0$ and it requires $\kappa\leq d^{1-\delta}$ .

4.1 The Hard Family We use a construction similar to that in [ 22 , Section 9.2] ,
with a cosine eigenvalue grid (Chebyshev nodes) and a random orthogonal rotation, while extending the spectral
interval from $[1,2]$ to $[1,\kappa]$ .
For $d\geq 2$ and $\kappa\geq 2$ , define $\theta_{i}=\frac{(i-\tfrac{1}{2})\pi}{d},\qquad\lambda_{i}=\frac{\kappa+1}{2}+\frac{\kappa-1}{2}\cos\theta_{i},\qquad\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}).$ (11) Draw $O\sim\operatorname{Haar}(\mathrm{O}(d))$ , independently
of the algorithm’s private randomness, and set $A=O\Lambda O^{\top}$ .
The associated potential and target are $f_{A}(x)=x^{\top}Ax/2$ and $\pi_{A}=N(0,A^{-1})$ . The spectrum is deterministic and may be supplied to the algorithm.
In particular, the Gaussian normalizing constant is known.
The unknown component is the random eigenbasis represented by $O$ . Remark 4.2 ( Comparison with $\kappa=2$ case). At $\kappa=2$ , the spectrum in
Eq. ( 11 ) recovers the original construction in [ 22 ] .
More generally, if $A_{2}$ denotes the original precision matrix
with the same rotation $O$ , then $A=(\kappa-1)A_{2}-(\kappa-2)I_{d}$ .
Thus one matrix–vector reply for either matrix determines
the corresponding reply for the other.
The proof compares $\sum_{i}\cos(n\theta_{i})\langle x,Oe_{i}\rangle^{2}$ with $\|x\|^{2}$ , where $n=2q+1$ .
For the output of a $q$ -query algorithm, the ratio is at most $d^{-\Omega(1)}$ in absolute value
(Lemma 4.4 ), whereas under the target the ratio of the means has absolute value $e^{-\Theta(n/\sqrt{\kappa})}$ (Lemma 4.5 ).
Hence the test works for $n$ up to order $\sqrt{\kappa}\log d$ , rather than $\log d$ as at $\kappa=2$ , which is the source of the factor $\sqrt{\kappa}$ in
Theorem 4.1 .

4.2 Properties of the Hard Family This subsection states the four lemmas used in the proof of Theorem 4.1 .
Lemma 4.3 places the output of any $q$ -query algorithm in a block Krylov space generated by independent
Gaussian vectors,
Lemma 4.4 bounds a quadratic form with cosine weights
uniformly over this space,
Lemma 4.5 evaluates the same quadratic form under the target, and
Lemma 4.6 verifies that the hard family is admissible.
All proofs are deferred to Appendix C . Krylov representation of the output. We first control the information revealed by adaptive queries via a standard Krylov argument.
The deferred-rotation argument of [ 22 , Lemma 9.2] uses the rotational symmetry
of the undisclosed eigenbasis to represent the algorithm’s
output in a block Krylov space generated by
independent Gaussian vectors. For vectors $g_{1},\ldots,g_{q+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ and $\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})$ , define $\mathcal{K}_{q}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{q+1})=\operatorname{span}\{\Lambda^{j}g_{a}:1\leq a\leq q+1,\ 0\leq j\leq q\}.$ Lemma 4.3 ( Krylov representation). Fix an integer $q\geq 0$ with $2q+1<d$ and let $X$ be the output. There exist independent vectors $g_{1},\ldots,g_{q+1}\sim N(0,I_{d})$ such that $O^{\top}X\in\mathcal{K}_{q}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{q+1})\qquad\text{almost surely}.$ The additional starting vector accounts for the
output, which may lie outside the span of the query points
and oracle replies.
Equivalently, the lemma gives a representation $O^{\top}X=\sum_{a=1}^{q+1}p_{a}(\Lambda)g_{a}$ ,
where each $p_{a}$ has degree at most $q$ .
The polynomial coefficients may depend on the entire
extended experiment.
We therefore control the output through an estimate that
holds simultaneously for every vector in $\mathcal{K}_{q}$ .
The proof of Lemma 4.3 is deferred to

For a query budget $q$ , set $n=2q+1,\qquad D_{n}=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\cos(n\theta_{1}),\ldots,\cos(n\theta_{d})\bigr).$ The choice of $n$ places this statistic above all frequencies

Appendix C.2 .
Since $\lambda_{i}$ is a nonconstant affine function of $\cos\theta_{i}$ , the space $\mathcal{K}_{q}$ is equivalently spanned

We now evaluate the same cosine statistic under the target Gaussian. Lemma 4.5 ( Moments under the target). Let $\kappa\geq 2$ , $U\sim N(0,\Lambda^{-1})$ , and $\varrho_{\kappa}=\tfrac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}$ .
For an integer $1\leq n<d$ , put $D_{n}=\operatorname{diag}(\cos(n\theta_{i}))$ , $M=\mathbb{E}\|U\|^{2}$ , and $\mu_{n}=\mathbb{E}[U^{\top}D_{n}U]$ .

C.4 Proof of Lemma 4.6 Proof of Lemma 4.6 . Since $0<\theta_{i}<\pi$ , we have $-1<\cos\theta_{i}<1$ ,
and hence $1<\lambda_{i}<\kappa$ .
As $A=O\Lambda O^{\top}$ with $O$ orthogonal, this gives $I_{d}\preceq A\preceq\kappa I_{d}$ . The potential $V_{A}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}x^{\top}Ax$ is $C^{\infty}$ with Hessian $\nabla^{2}V_{A}(x)=A$ .
Thus it is $1$ -strongly convex and $\kappa$ -smooth.
Moreover, $V_{A}(0)=0$ and $V_{A}(x)\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}>0$ for $x\neq 0$ ,
so $0$ is its unique minimizer. The associated target is $N(0,A^{-1})$ .
Gaussian integration gives $Z_{A}=(2\pi)^{d/2}(\det A)^{-1/2}=(2\pi)^{d/2}(\det\Lambda)^{-1/2}.$ Since $\Lambda$ is fixed, $Z_{A}$ is independent of $O$ and is known whenever the spectrum is public.
