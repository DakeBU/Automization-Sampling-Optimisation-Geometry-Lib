[[S4.SS1]]

4.1 The Hard Family

[[S4.SS1.p1]]

[[S4.SS1.p1.1]] We use a construction similar to that in [22, Section 9.2], with a cosine eigenvalue grid (Chebyshev nodes) and a random orthogonal rotation, while extending the spectral interval from $[1,2]$ to $[1,\kappa]$ . For $d\geq 2$ and $\kappa\geq 2$ , define

[[S4.E11]]

$\theta_{i}=\frac{(i-\tfrac{1}{2})\pi}{d},\qquad\lambda_{i}=\frac{\kappa+1}{2}+\frac{\kappa-1}{2}\cos\theta_{i},\qquad\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}).$ (11)

[[S4.SS1.p1.2]] Draw $O\sim\operatorname{Haar}(\mathrm{O}(d))$ , independently of the algorithm’s private randomness, and set $A=O\Lambda O^{\top}$ . The associated potential and target are $f_{A}(x)=x^{\top}Ax/2$ and $\pi_{A}=N(0,A^{-1})$ .

[[S4.SS1.p2]]

[[S4.SS1.p2.1]] The spectrum is deterministic and may be supplied to the algorithm. In particular, the Gaussian normalizing constant is known. The unknown component is the random eigenbasis represented by $O$ .

[[S4.Thmtheorem2]]

Remark 4.2 (Comparison with $\kappa=2$ case).

[[S4.Thmtheorem2.p1]]

[[S4.Thmtheorem2.p1.1]] At $\kappa=2$ , the spectrum in Eq. (11) recovers the original construction in [22]. More generally, if $A_{2}$ denotes the original precision matrix with the same rotation $O$ , then $A=(\kappa-1)A_{2}-(\kappa-2)I_{d}$ . Thus one matrix–vector reply for either matrix determines the corresponding reply for the other. The proof compares $\sum_{i}\cos(n\theta_{i})\langle x,Oe_{i}\rangle^{2}$ with $\|x\|^{2}$ , where $n=2q+1$ . For the output of a $q$ -query algorithm, the ratio is at most $d^{-\Omega(1)}$ in absolute value (Lemma 4.4), whereas under the target the ratio of the means has absolute value $e^{-\Theta(n/\sqrt{\kappa})}$ (Lemma 4.5). Hence the test works for $n$ up to order $\sqrt{\kappa}\log d$ , rather than $\log d$ as at $\kappa=2$ , which is the source of the factor $\sqrt{\kappa}$ in Theorem 4.1.
