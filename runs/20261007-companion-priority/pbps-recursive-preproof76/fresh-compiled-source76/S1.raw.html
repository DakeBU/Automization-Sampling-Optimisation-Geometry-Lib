1 Introduction

We study the problem of sampling from the probability measure μ⁡(d​x)=Z−1​e−V⁡(x)​d​x\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=Z^{-1}e^{-V(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x on ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d}, where V∈C2​(ℝd)V\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}) satisfies, for 0<α≤β0<\alpha\leq\beta,

The main result of this paper is the following.

Theorem (Main theorem; informal).

Assume (1.1), and suppose that the initial law μ0\mu_{0} satisfies the warm start condition in order-22 Rényi divergence: D2(μ0∥μ)=O(1)D_{2}(\mu_{0}\|\mu)=O(1). For every 0<ε<1/40<\varepsilon<1/4, there is a randomized algorithm that, given X0∼μ0X_{0}\sim\mu_{0} and query access to ∇V\nabla V, returns X^∼μ^\widehat{X}\sim\widehat{\mu} satisfying

TV⁡(μ^,μ)≤ε,\operatorname{TV}(\widehat{\mu},\mu)\leq\varepsilon\,,

and whose expected number of queries to ∇V\nabla V is at most

O~​(κ​d1/4).\widetilde{O}\bigl(\sqrt{\kappa}\,d^{1/4}\bigr)\,.

Here O~\widetilde{O} hides universal powers of logarithms in dd, κ\kappa, and 1/ε1/\varepsilon.

The precise statement and parameter choices appear in Theorems 4.3 and 4.4.

We compare our result with high-accuracy samplers, that is, samplers whose complexity depends polylogarithmically on 1/ε1/\varepsilon. Under (1.1), the state of the art until recently was the Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm (MALA) [1, 10, 27] and the proximal sampler [6, 15], which achieved O~​(κ​d1/2)\widetilde{O}(\kappa d^{1/2}) complexity. Very recently, Chen et al. [8] used the first-order rejection sampling (FORS) mechanism to develop a method for exact simulation of diffusions. Together with accelerated entropic hypocoercivity [19, 22], this yielded a high-accuracy sampler with complexity O~​(κ2/3​d1/3)\widetilde{O}(\kappa^{2/3}d^{1/3}). Also, Lu and Luo [20] introduced windowed thinning, an exact simulation method for the bouncy particle sampler (BPS) and the zigzag sampler. Their estimates give expected complexities of O~​(κ1/2​d)\widetilde{O}(\kappa^{1/2}d) gradient queries for BPS and O~​(κ​d5/4)\widetilde{O}(\kappa d^{5/4}) partial derivative queries for zigzag, from a warm start. These results were significant in part because they constituted progress toward acceleration for log-concave sampling.

From a warm start, our main theorem achieves complexity O~​(κ1/2​d1/4)\widetilde{O}(\kappa^{1/2}d^{1/4}). It matches the condition number dependence of BPS while improving its dimension dependence, and always improves upon the computational cost of the zigzag sampler (even if we consider one gradient query to be dd times as expensive as a partial derivative query). It also improves over all other prior results in both parameters. In particular, it achieves full acceleration together with d1/4d^{1/4} dependence on dimension.

In a companion work [7], we will show how to obtain a warm start using O~​(κ7/6​d1/6)\widetilde{O}(\kappa^{7/6}d^{1/6}) gradient queries. We do not discuss the warm start question further in this paper.

Proximal Bouncy Particle Sampler.

Our algorithm, the Proximal Bouncy Particle Sampler (Proximal BPS), combines two ideas. The proximal sampler [17] breaks the sampling problem into a sequence of easier local problems by introducing a Gaussian perturbation of the current position. The bouncy particle sampler [5] explores a distribution by moving a particle along straight lines, with random changes of direction chosen to preserve the desired distribution. We bring these ideas together by designing local particle motions that retain a memory of previous steps. This memory acts as momentum, allowing the algorithm to make sustained progress across successive local problems and giving the accelerated convergence rate in our main theorem. The local motions follow explicitly computable curved paths, with occasional “bounces” that correct for the shape of the target distribution. A gradient evaluation at a nearby reference point makes these corrections inexpensive: since the gradient varies little over a small region, only a small number of bounces is needed. Balancing the size of the local problems against the cost of these corrections yields the O~​(κ1/2​d1/4)\widetilde{O}(\kappa^{1/2}d^{1/4}) query bound. We feel that the construction of the sampler (with the help of GPT-5.6 Sol) contains some surprising ingredients. We describe the construction and its geometry in Section 3.1, and give the implementation and complexity analysis in Section 4.

Related work.

This work makes progress on the complexity of log-concave sampling, for which the textbook [11] provides an introduction and overview.

The proximal sampling framework is from Lee, Shen, and Tian [17], with subsequent analyses and implementations in [6, 9, 15]. These methods alternate independent draws of the position and the auxiliary variable from their conditional distributions. Our update reflects the auxiliary point through the current position, a step related to the Gaussian overrelaxation method of Neal [25]. We combine this reflection with a local piecewise deterministic particle motion run for half of its oscillation period. As we shall explain, this helps useful information to persist across iterations.

The rule for the bounces builds on the BPS of Bouchard-Côté, Vollmer, and Doucet [5]. The use of a quadratic reference potential to generate curved paths, with bounces correcting for the remaining force, is adapted from the boomerang sampler of Bierkens, Grazzi, Kamatani, and Roberts [4]. This separation of the force also fits the general framework for Hamiltonian piecewise deterministic Markov processes of Andrieu, Durmus, Nüsken, and Roussel [2].

Existing convergence analyses of these processes [2, 13, 21] concern continuous-time dynamics with repeated random refreshment of the velocity. Our local motion has no such refreshment: it stops after a half-period, and convergence comes from combining it with the auxiliary reflection step with occasional resampling. In particular, the local motion alone need not converge to its conditional equilibrium. Thus, the continuous-time results do not directly give a convergence guarantee for our discrete chain. Our analysis instead adapts the modified L2L^{2} hypocoercivity method of Dolbeault, Mouhot, and Schmeiser [14], in the sharpened formulation of Fan, Li, and Lu [16], to this discrete setting. The technical details of the hypocoercivity mechanism in Appendices B and C should be of interest to specialists.
