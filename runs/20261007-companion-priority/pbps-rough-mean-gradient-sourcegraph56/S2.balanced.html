2 Preliminaries

2.1 The standard Bouncy Particle Sampler

We first recall the usual Bouncy Particle Sampler (BPS) for readers unfamiliar with the construction [5]. It is a piecewise-deterministic Markov process on phase space (x,p)∈ℝd×ℝd(x,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}, where pp denotes velocity (equivalently, momentum for unit mass). Its invariant distribution is μ⊗φ\mu\otimes\varphi, where φ=𝒩⁡(0,I)\varphi=\mathcal{N}(0,I). Between events, the particle moves along a straight line with constant pp:

x˙t=pt,p˙t=0.\dot{x}_{t}=p_{t}\,,\hskip 20.00003pt\dot{p}_{t}=0\,. (2.1)

At state (x,p)(x,p), a bounce occurs at rate

λBPS(x,p)≔[p⊤∇V(x)]+.\lambda_{\rm BPS}(x,p)\coloneqq[p^{\top}\nabla V(x)]_{+}\,. (2.2)

Equivalently, conditional on the current state and in the absence of a velocity refresh, the next bounce time τb\tau_{\rm b} satisfies

ℙ(τb>t∣x,p)=exp{−∫0t[p⊤∇V(x+sp)]+ds}.\mathbb{P}(\tau_{\rm b}>t\mid x,p)=\exp\Bigl\{-\int_{0}^{t}[p^{\top}\nabla V(x+sp)]_{+}\,\mathrm{d}s\Bigr\}\,. (2.3)

At a bounce, the position is unchanged and the velocity is specularly reflected against the gradient. For non-zero h∈ℝdh\in\mathbb{R}^{d}, set

Rh≔I−2​h​h⊤‖h‖2,R0≔I.R_{h}\coloneqq I-2\,\frac{hh^{\top}}{\|h\|^{2}}\,,\hskip 20.00003ptR_{0}\coloneqq I\,. (2.4)

The bounce update is p←R∇V​(x)​pp\leftarrow R_{\nabla V(x)}p. It preserves ‖p‖\|p\|, leaves the component tangent to a level set unchanged, and reverses the normal component, since (Rh​p)⊤​h=−p⊤​h(R_{h}p)^{\top}h=-p^{\top}h. Thus, the particle travels freely while moving down the potential and bounces, at a rate equal to its instantaneous uphill directional derivative, when it moves up the potential. Proximal BPS reuses this bounce mechanism, applied to a localized conditional target; the construction is given in Section 3.

The standard bouncy particle sampler also uses independent velocity refreshes to ensure ergodicity, at which pp is redrawn from φ\varphi. Writing

(Πp​f)​(x,p)≔∫ℝdf⁡(x,p′)​φ​(d​p′),(\mathsf{\Pi}_{p}f)(x,p)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,p^{\prime})\,\varphi(\mathrm{d}p^{\prime})\,,

the full generator of BPS is

(𝖫BPS​f)​(x,p)=p⊤∇xf(x,p)+[p⊤∇V(x)]+{f(x,R∇V​(x)p)−f(x,p)}+γ(Πpf−f)(x,p).\begin{split}(\mathsf{L}_{\rm BPS}f)(x,p)&=p^{\top}\nabla_{x}f(x,p)+[p^{\top}\nabla V(x)]_{+}\{f(x,R_{\nabla V(x)}p)-f(x,p)\}+\gamma(\mathsf{\Pi}_{p}f-f)(x,p)\,.\end{split} (2.5)

For every refresh rate γ≥0\gamma\geq 0, the process with generator (2.5) leaves μ⊗φ\mu\otimes\varphi invariant [5], and its position marginal is the desired target μ\mu.

A standard way to simulate the inhomogeneous event clock (2.3) is Poisson thinning [18]. Suppose that, along the current deterministic segment, the rate λ⁡(⋅)\lambda(\cdot) is bounded above by a constant λ¯\bar{\lambda}. Generate candidate times from a homogeneous Poisson process of rate λ¯\bar{\lambda} and accept a candidate at time tt with probability λ⁡(t)/λ¯\lambda(t)/\bar{\lambda}; a rejected candidate causes no jump, and the deterministic trajectory continues. The accepted times then have rate λ⁡(⋅)\lambda(\cdot), so this procedure simulates the event clock exactly. Note that only at each candidate time, one needs to query the potential gradients. If no convenient dominating bound is available, accepting instead with probability min⁡{1,λ⁡(t)/λ¯}\min\{1,\lambda(t)/\bar{\lambda}\} exactly simulates the truncated rate min⁡{λ⁡(t),λ¯}\min\{\lambda(t),\bar{\lambda}\}; the only approximation is then the replacement of the original rate by its truncation. This is used in Section 4.2.

2.2 Proximal augmentation and the restricted Gaussian oracle

Our algorithm operates on the augmented distribution underlying the proximal sampler of Lee, Shen, and Tian [17]. For a scale η∈(0,1/β]\eta\in(0,1/\beta], consider the joint law

πη​(d​x​d​y)∝exp⁡{−V⁡(x)−‖x−y‖22​η}​d​x​d​y.\pi_{\eta}(\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y)\propto\exp\Bigl\{-V(x)-\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2\eta}\Bigr\}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\,. (2.6)

The original proximal sampler algorithm performs Gibbs sampling on πη\pi_{\eta}. Namely, under πη\pi_{\eta} one may generate the joint state as

X∼μ,Z∼𝒩⁡(0,I)​independently,Y=X+η​Z.X\sim\mu\,,\hskip 20.00003ptZ\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\ \text{independently}\,,\hskip 20.00003ptY=X+\sqrt{\eta}\,Z\,. (2.7)

Its XX-marginal is πηX=μ\pi_{\eta}^{X}=\mu. Write πηY\pi_{\eta}^{Y} for the YY-marginal, so that πηY​(d​y)∝e−Uη​(y)​d​y\pi_{\eta}^{Y}(\mathrm{d}y)\propto e^{-U_{\eta}(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y; by (2.7), πηY=μ∗𝒩⁡(0,η​I)\pi_{\eta}^{Y}=\mu*\mathcal{N}(0,\eta I) is a Gaussian smoothing of the target. We use superscripts for marginal and conditional laws; in particular,

πηY|X=x=𝒩⁡(x,η​I),πηX|Y=y​(d​x)∝exp⁡{−V⁡(x)−‖x−y‖22​η}​d​x.\pi_{\eta}^{Y\mid X=x}=\mathcal{N}(x,\eta I)\,,\hskip 20.00003pt\pi_{\eta}^{X\mid Y=y}(\mathrm{d}x)\propto\exp\Bigl\{-V(x)-\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2\eta}\Bigr\}\,\mathrm{d}x\,. (2.8)

Gibbs sampling alternates exact draws from the two conditional laws in (2.8). The scale η\eta controls the strength of the quadratic coupling potential. Decreasing η\eta makes XX and YY more tightly coupled and the conditional law of XX given YY more localized and better conditioned, which reduces the cost of conditional sampling. The resulting transitions are also more local, however, and generally require more Gibbs sampling iterations. Thus η\eta balances the cost of each iteration against the number of iterations. The convergence and query complexity are studied further in [6, 9, 15].

The next proposition collects the basic geometric properties of the augmented distribution used throughout the paper: the curvature and concentration of the conditional law, the curvature of the YY-marginal, and a reflection symmetry of the joint law.

Proposition 2.1 (Geometry of the joint law).

The joint law (2.6) has the following properties.

  1. (i)

    For every y∈ℝdy\in\mathbb{R}^{d}, the conditional law πηX|Y=y\pi_{\eta}^{X\mid Y=y} has potential (that is, negative log-density up to an additive constant)

    Vy​(x)≔V⁡(x)+‖x−y‖22​η.V_{y}(x)\coloneqq V(x)+\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2\eta}\,. (2.9)

    This conditional potential satisfies

    (α+η−1)​I⪯∇2Vy​(x)⪯(β+η−1)​I,(\alpha+\eta^{-1})I\preceq\nabla^{2}V_{y}(x)\preceq(\beta+\eta^{-1})I\,, (2.10)

    so its condition number is at most (1+β​η)/(1+α​η)≤2(1+\beta\eta)/(1+\alpha\eta)\leq 2. Consequently, for every smooth ff,

    VarπηX|Y=y⁡(f)≤η1+α​η​𝔼πηX|Y=y​‖∇f‖2,\Var_{\pi_{\eta}^{X\mid Y=y}}(f)\leq\frac{\eta}{1+\alpha\eta}\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\eta}^{X\mid Y=y}}\|\nabla f\|^{2}\,, (2.11)

    and

    η1+β​η​I⪯CovπηX|Y=y​(X)⪯η1+α​η​I.\frac{\eta}{1+\beta\eta}I\preceq\mathrm{Cov}_{\pi_{\eta}^{X\mid Y=y}}(X)\preceq\frac{\eta}{1+\alpha\eta}I\,. (2.12)
  2. (ii)

    The YY-marginal potential satisfies

    α1+α​η​I⪯∇2Uη​(y)⪯β1+β​η​I.\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha\eta}I\preceq\nabla^{2}U_{\eta}(y)\preceq\frac{\beta}{1+\beta\eta}I\,. (2.13)
  3. (iii)

    The state-space reflection

    𝖴:(x,y)↦(x,2​x−y)\mathsf{U}:(x,y)\mapsto(x,2x-y) (2.14)

    preserves πη\pi_{\eta} and is an involution.

The Hessian bound in part (i) follows immediately from (1.1); the Poincaré and covariance bounds then follow from the standard log-concavity facts (D.6) and Lemma D.3 in Appendix D, applied with m=α+η−1m=\alpha+\eta^{-1} and L=β+η−1L=\beta+\eta^{-1}. The marginal curvature bound in part (ii) is derived in Appendix D, at (D.10). The reflection statement follows directly from (2.6).

We use the standard proximal map convention of Moreau [24]:

proxη​V⁡(y)≔arg⁡minx∈ℝd​{V⁡(x)+‖x−y‖22​η}.\operatorname{prox}_{\eta V}(y)\coloneqq\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl\{V(x)+\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2\eta}\Bigr\}\,. (2.15)

The minimizer is unique under (1.1). The objective minimized here is exactly the conditional potential (2.9), so proxη​V⁡(y)\operatorname{prox}_{\eta V}(y) is the mode of πηX|Y=y\pi_{\eta}^{X\mid Y=y}; the conditional draw is in this sense a stochastic analogue of a proximal step, whence the terminology arises. This exact minimizer is used below only in the analysis; the implementable algorithm never evaluates it and does not assume access to a proximal oracle. We use the following conditional sampling primitive, introduced in [17].

Definition 2.2 (Restricted Gaussian oracle).

For η>0\eta>0, a restricted Gaussian oracle (RGO) takes input y∈ℝdy\in\mathbb{R}^{d} and, using fresh randomness, returns a sample with law πηX|Y=y\pi_{\eta}^{X\mid Y=y}.
